> **From the archive**  
> Originally published on Siklab Serye · January 8, 2024

A new year arrives with possibilities, hopes, and the pressure to move quickly. In our eagerness to seize it, we can forget the value of taking things slowly enough to notice them.

For 2024, I wanted progress with more purpose and less urgency.

## Be present before being productive

Start with the reason behind a goal, not only the result. A few quiet minutes, a gratitude journal, or a deliberate pause can bring attention back to the present.

Make plans, but leave room for rest and spontaneity. Focusing on one thing at a time is not a failure of ambition. It is a way of giving the work—and the life around it—more care.

## Keep what creates space

Our surroundings can either carry us or crowd us. Simplifying a room, stepping away from a screen, or letting go of something that no longer serves a purpose can make room for what does.

The same is true of relationships. Time with the people who matter is not an interruption to the plan. It is part of the point.

## Let curiosity set the direction

Learning does not always need to lead to a credential or a measurable return. A new hobby, skill, language, or question can be worthwhile simply because it keeps curiosity alive.

Reflection belongs in that process. Looking back at what worked, what did not, and what changed gives us a chance to adapt instead of repeating ourselves automatically.

## Notice the small wins

Progress is often easier to see in retrospect. Celebrating small milestones keeps the journey from disappearing behind the destination.

There is joy in ordinary things, too: morning coffee, a quiet sunset, a task completed with attention. These moments may not look like achievement, but they are part of a life being lived.

As the year begins, I want to measure success less by speed and more by depth—by what I understand, what I notice, and how fully I am present for it. Embrace the pace. A small step can still be a step toward something meaningful.

## Updated for Field Notes · 2026

The original post was written as a New Year list, and I can see the tension in that format now. It argues for mindfulness, rest, curiosity, and ordinary joy, then arranges them into another program to follow. Even a gentler life can become a project plan if every quiet practice has to prove that it improves us.

I would keep the central idea and loosen its grip. Pace is not valuable only because moving more slowly produces better work. An unclaimed hour does not need to return as a skill, a cleaner room, a completed journal, or a more focused version of the person who entered it.

Field Notes itself emerged from time spent experimenting without a guaranteed result. The later essay about boredom makes the connection more clearly: empty attention sometimes moves sideways. It combines unrelated interests, tries a tool without a business case, or stays with a question longer than usefulness requires. That is not structured flexibility. It is what becomes possible when the structure briefly stops making demands.

The morning coffee and quiet sunset in the 2024 piece matter for the same reason. Their value is not that they are techniques for managing the year. They are moments that can remain ordinary and still be fully noticed.

What I understand differently now is that embracing the pace is less about selecting the correct speed than protecting some part of life from constant conversion into progress. A small step can be meaningful. So can a pause that leads nowhere measurable.
