A passage from Morgan Housel's *Same as Ever* was recently circulated to executives. The argument, drawing on an observation attributed to Jeff Bezos, was that even very good jobs contain unpleasant parts. Stress, uncertainty, conflicting incentives, bureaucracy, hassle and doubt are not necessarily signs that something has gone wrong. They can be the overhead cost of getting ahead.

I understand the argument. It is sensible, bracing and probably useful for anyone who expects worthwhile work to arrive without inconvenience.

My less serene reaction was: I have paid the overhead. Where is the reward?

This is not an objection to difficulty. Most work with any consequence includes moments when the answer is unclear, the stakes are real and the people involved want different things. Learning a difficult skill is frustrating. Taking responsibility for a larger problem is stressful. Building support for an unfamiliar idea is slow. The friction is not incidental to the work; it is part of how capability and judgment are formed.

But difficulty is a poor quality standard. An experience does not become developmental simply because it is exhausting. A process does not become necessary because everyone has learned to complain about it in the same resigned tone.

Organizations are especially talented at confusing these two kinds of discomfort.

## What is the friction purchasing?

Productive friction has an exchange rate. It costs time, energy or certainty, but it buys something meaningful in return.

Perhaps the work expands your scope. You are asked to think beyond your function, understand a broader system or take responsibility for a result rather than a task. Perhaps it gives you real authority, not merely accountability with a decorative title attached. It might deepen a capability, increase your visibility, improve your compensation or move an important piece of work forward. At minimum, it should create a credible next step.

The return does not have to be immediate. Many worthwhile investments are uncomfortable before they are legible. The first months in a larger role can feel like a sequence of public reminders that you do not yet know enough. A difficult project may not pay off in recognition but can leave you with better judgment, stronger relationships and proof that you can operate at a new level.

Still, there should be a plausible account of what the difficulty is for.

If a person repeatedly absorbs stress without gaining scope, authority, capability, compensation, visibility or progress, the organization is not charging overhead. It may simply be transferring the cost of its dysfunction to the employee.

Calling that experience “growth” is convenient. It is also a little like describing a delayed flight as an advanced course in aviation.

## Waste often wears serious clothing

Organizational waste rarely announces itself as waste. It arrives as governance, alignment, stakeholder management or one final round of input. All of those things can be valuable. They can also become respectable names for indecision.

A useful review catches a risk. An unnecessary review redistributes responsibility so thoroughly that no one can identify who made the decision. Healthy debate tests an idea. Dysfunctional debate repeats because the organization has not decided which principle should resolve the disagreement. A process can protect quality; it can also protect people from being visibly wrong.

The distinction is not that productive friction feels good and waste feels bad. Both can be unpleasant. The distinction is whether the resistance improves the work or merely delays it.

Productive friction clarifies trade-offs. Waste recreates the same argument with a larger mailing list.

Productive friction makes the decision stronger. Waste makes the decision harder to locate.

Productive friction leaves the organization more capable the next time. Waste becomes the way things are done next time.

That last feature matters. Temporary inefficiency is often understandable. A new team, unusual project or genuine crisis can require awkward coordination. The concern is when the workaround becomes infrastructure. People begin designing their behavior around the dysfunction, adding pre-meetings before meetings and private approval chains before the official approval chain. Eventually the organization develops an impressive operational system for surviving itself.

## Tolerance is not the only leadership skill

Housel's larger point about overhead is valuable because it corrects a common fantasy: that success eventually removes uncertainty, politics, tedious work and doubt. It does not. Seniority may change the form of the difficulty, but it does not grant immunity from it.

The idea becomes less useful when leaders apply it only downward. It is easy to tell people that friction builds character. It is harder to ask whether the friction exists because leaders have avoided choosing priorities, resolving ownership or ending a process that no longer serves its purpose.

Good leadership should help people distinguish between the discomfort worth carrying and the waste that should be removed. That requires more than encouragement. It requires decisions.

Leaders can clarify who has authority. They can reduce duplicate reviews, make trade-offs explicit, close decisions that have already been made and stop inviting feedback from people who do not own the outcome. They can notice when “resilience” has become the preferred solution to poor system design.

There will always be overhead. The point is not to create a workplace without stress, uncertainty or inconvenience. That promise would be dishonest, and probably unbearable in its own bureaucratic way.

The better standard is proportionality. Is the overhead appropriate to the ambition? Is it producing a better decision, a stronger person or a more capable organization? Are the people paying it able to see a credible return?

If the answer is yes, the difficulty may be worth it. If the answer is no, endurance is not a strategy. It is an invoice that has gone unpaid.
