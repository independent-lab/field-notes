I miss things that were inconvenient, which is not the same as wanting inconvenience back.

The distinction matters. Modern convenience is excellent. Music on demand is better than music unavailable. A film available tonight is more useful than one requiring an expedition and luck. Search is preferable to ignorance, and a map that knows where you are has obvious advantages over one that becomes philosophical at the first wrong turn.

Convenience solves real problems. It also has a habit of clearing away the experiences that formed around those problems.

The inconvenience was rarely the thing worth keeping. What grew beside it sometimes was.

## The shape around the object

Physical media required space, care and decisions. A magazine arrived as a complete object rather than a stream of individually optimized pieces. Scheduled television expected you at a particular time and offered no apology if you were elsewhere. Stores limited discovery to what happened to be stocked, visible and affordable that day.

None of this was inherently noble. Limited choice often meant you could not get what you wanted. Scheduled programming was somebody else’s timetable. Owning things meant storing and cleaning them.

But these limitations gave experiences a shape.

When access was not immediate, anticipation had room to develop. When choices were limited, selection carried more weight. When an object occupied physical space, it accumulated evidence of use. When a program happened at a set time, watching it could become an appointment rather than one interchangeable option in an endless library.

The media was surrounded by ritual: looking, choosing, waiting, returning, lending, keeping. The ritual did not make every book, record, magazine or broadcast better. It made the encounter larger than the moment of consumption.

Convenience compresses that encounter. This is usually the service we asked it to provide.

## Discovery without a perfect match

The old limits also created a peculiar kind of discovery. You found something because it was available, not because it was the ideal answer to a detailed preference profile.

Availability is a crude recommendation system. It can be frustrating and unfair. It can also push attention toward an option that would lose every personalized ranking but prove interesting once encountered. A shelf, a broadcast schedule or a stack of magazines does not know you well enough to keep confirming you.

Modern recommendation systems can introduce people to wonderful things. Their weakness is not that they always narrow taste. They are simply very good at making the next choice feel continuous with the last one. Discovery becomes smoother and perhaps less surprising.

There was another form of accidental discovery in simply being unable to skip. You heard the less immediate track. You read around the article you had come for. You watched what was on because the preferred alternative was not. Much of this material deserved to be skipped. Some of it expanded the boundaries of what you thought you liked.

A limited menu could disappoint you into curiosity.

## Attachment needs somewhere to land

Ownership changed the relationship in a different way. A physical object could become familiar through placement and repetition. You knew where it lived. Its wear recorded contact. Keeping it involved a series of small recommitments, even if that commitment was only deciding not to throw it away during another attempt to organize the room.

Digital access offers a larger world with a smaller footprint. That is a remarkable trade. It also makes attachment more abstract. A favorite thing can be one entry among millions, summoned instantly and then returned to an invisible catalog. The experience remains real, but it leaves fewer marks on the room around it.

This is not an argument for accumulating objects as proof of feeling. Possession can become burden, identity performance or very expensive shelving. It is simply an observation that attachment often grows through repeated contact, and physical inconvenience supplied contact almost by accident.

## Friction was doing more than one job

We tend to evaluate friction by asking whether it delayed the desired result. If it did, remove it. This is sensible engineering. The complication is that a delay can perform several jobs at once.

Waiting can be waste, but it can also create anticipation. Scarcity can be exclusion, but it can also force a choice. A fixed schedule can be restrictive, but it can also coordinate attention. Ownership can be cumbersome, but it can also create care. Browsing can be inefficient, but it can also expose the person doing the browsing to something unrequested.

When we remove friction, we remove all of its jobs together. The primary result improves. The secondary experiences may disappear quietly because no metric was assigned to them.

I do not want a campaign to restore inconvenience. I do not want fewer choices selected by somebody who insists the limitation will build character. I would not trade the reach of modern culture for the ritual of acquiring a smaller portion of it.

But I miss the experiences that gathered around certain difficult things. The anticipation before access. The commitment after choosing. The discovery caused by imperfect availability. The attachment produced by an object staying long enough to become part of a place.

The useful question is not whether the old inconvenience was better. Usually it was not. It is whether convenience removed something else along with the obstacle, and whether that missing experience can be recovered without rebuilding the obstacle itself.
