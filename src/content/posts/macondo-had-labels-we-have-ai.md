I was watching Netflix’s *One Hundred Years of Solitude* when the insomnia plague arrived in Macondo.

At first, the loss of sleep seems almost useful. The town gains time. People work through the night. Then the stranger effect begins: memory starts to disappear. Names go first, then the uses of ordinary things, then the shared understanding that makes a world recognizable.

So the people of Macondo begin labeling everything.

A cow is given a sign explaining that it is a cow. The sign has to say that she must be milked, and that the milk is used in coffee. It is not enough to preserve the name of the object. They have to preserve the chain of meaning around it.

And somewhere in the middle of watching all this, I realized I had forgotten much of the book.

## My own small insomnia plague

I remembered the Buendías. I remembered Macondo. I remembered that the same names kept returning—José Arcadio, Aureliano, another José Arcadio, another Aureliano—until the family tree began to feel less like a guide and more like part of the spell.

I remembered the atmosphere more than the sequence. Heat, rain, solitude, impossible things treated as ordinary. I even vaguely remembered a gay storyline, though I could no longer place it confidently or tell you exactly what happened.

What I did not remember was how one event led to another. Whole lives had collapsed into impressions. Characters I must once have followed closely had become names without labels.

There was something funny about it. I was watching a story about a town losing its memory while experiencing my own quieter version of the same problem. Macondo had forgotten what objects were for. I had forgotten what happened in *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

The joke improved when I noticed the obvious solution sitting beside me.

I could ask AI.

## A label for everything

I could have asked for the entire plot in order. I could have requested a family tree, an explanation of every repeated name, a list of the details I was misremembering and a neat account of the storyline hovering at the edge of my memory.

In a few seconds, the book could have been reconstructed around me.

That possibility felt strangely close to Macondo’s labels. The town wrote language onto the physical world so that knowledge would remain available when memory failed. We have built far more elaborate versions of the same idea.

We take photographs because a moment may not stay vivid. We keep messages because conversations disappear. We put documents in cloud storage, trusting that a filename and a search box can return something years after the mind has misplaced it. We build digital archives larger than any personal memory could hold. Search lets us recover the fact we once knew, the article we once read, the name that would not come.

Increasingly, AI sits above all of this as a new kind of label maker. It can search, sort, summarize and connect. It can take scattered fragments—a date, a place, half a sentence—and make the past legible again. If we give it access to enough of our material, it may remember where we put things better than we do.

This is useful. I do not want to pretend otherwise. Forgetting can be frustrating, wasteful and sometimes frightening. External memory has always extended what a person can keep: notes, books, calendars, photographs, recordings, databases. AI belongs to that long tradition, even if it can speak back in a way the old archive could not.

But a label is not the thing it labels.

## Retrieval is not remembering

I decided not to ask AI to reconstruct the whole novel.

I wanted some memories of the book to return naturally while I watched. I wanted a face, a room or a sentence to disturb something that had been sitting quietly in me for years. I wanted the surprise of recognition—not simply the delivery of information.

That kind of return is different from retrieval.

Retrieval gives us the record. Remembering gives the record a place inside us. It may arrive with an old feeling, with embarrassment at who we were, with affection for a room that no longer exists, or with the sudden realization that an event means something different now. Memory is unreliable partly because it is alive. It changes as we do.

AI can help recover the facts of our past. It can surface the photograph, summarize the exchange, arrange the timeline and remind us what came next. It may even find patterns across our lives that we never noticed.

What it cannot decide is what any of it means to us.

That authority still belongs to the person remembering. A machine can retrieve the message; it cannot determine whether the message is tender, painful, trivial or unfinished. It can tell us that we were there. It cannot settle who we became because of it.

There is also a small pleasure in not recovering everything at once. Watching the series with an incomplete memory lets the story occupy two times. I am meeting it as it appears now, while occasionally encountering the person who read the novel years ago. Sometimes that earlier reader sends a clear signal. Sometimes only a vague familiarity rises. Sometimes nothing returns.

The gaps are part of the experience.

Macondo’s labels were an emergency defense against a world becoming meaningless. Our tools are more powerful, more searchable and increasingly conversational, but the temptation is similar: if everything can be stored and summoned, perhaps nothing will truly be lost.

Perhaps. But a life without loss is not necessarily a life with memory. It may simply be a perfectly indexed archive.

I am glad the archive exists. I will use it. I will ask AI for names, dates, connections and the details that have fallen out of reach. I just do not want its answer to replace every uncertain return, every delayed recognition, every private act of making meaning from what remains.

Because perhaps there should still be parts of our lives that aren’t retrieved. They are remembered.
